# Documents are searched in PostgreSQL, by what their passages mean

A document an admin uploads is split into passages, and OpenAI's embedding model says what each passage means, as a list of numbers. The passages and their meanings are kept in PostgreSQL, in a column of pgvector's, and a question finds the passages whose meaning is closest to its own by a query that reads all of them.

The embedding model is `text-embedding-3-small`, which gives 1,536 numbers for a text. The column is that size. The model is a setting, and what it gives is not: another model gives other numbers, so changing it means a migration and processing every document again.

A passage is about 400 tokens, measured as 1,600 characters, and starts with up to 200 characters of how the one before it ended. It ends where a paragraph does when it can, or else where a sentence does.

## Considered Options

- **A vector database of its own, or OpenSearch.** One more thing to run, pay for, back up, and keep in step with the documents' rows. The passages belong to rows the database already has, are deleted with them by a foreign key, and are emptied with them by the demo reset.
- **An index for finding the closest passages.** pgvector has two, and both find the closest only nearly: a search may miss a passage. There are at most 20 documents of at most 300 passages, which is 6,000 rows, and reading all of them is exact and takes milliseconds. An index is for a table too large to read through.
- **Spring AI's store for pgvector.** It keeps passages in a table of its own shape, with what tells them apart in a JSON column. Here a passage is a row with a foreign key to its document, and which documents a search may read is a join on the document's vehicle line and model year, which the database then holds to, and not a filter over JSON.
- **The larger embedding model.** It gives 3,072 numbers and costs six and a half times as much. With a few dozen pages to search, the cases with known answers are what would show that the small one finds the wrong passage, and they are where to look before paying for more.
- **Counting a passage's size in tokens.** That takes a tokenizer and the model's own vocabulary. The model takes texts twenty times a passage's size, so nothing depends on the count being exact, and four characters to a token is what English comes to.

## Consequences

- The database's image is PostgreSQL with pgvector, in every place the database runs. A database in which the extension was created does not start on an image without it.
- What a search may read is decided in SQL, by the document's vehicle line and model year, before any passage reaches the model.
- A document is read as at most 300 passages. One with more text fails, and says so. A PDF can hold many times that much text in 2 MB, so reading one stops as soon as it has more.
- Reading a file and asking the model take seconds, and are done by the worker, in a job. A document that is being read says so, which is written apart from the job's own work.
- A PDF is given 30 seconds to be read, and its document fails when they are over. It is read by [a process of its own](0021-a-pdf-is-read-in-a-process-of-its-own-which-java-ends-in-the-workers-place.md), which the worker ends when the 30 seconds are over, and the worker goes on to its next job. One PDF is read at a time, so the worker never has more than one such process, and has none once it is started again. Until there was that process, PDFBox read in the worker, on a thread of its own, and did not stop when it was told to: a reading whose time was over was left to end by itself, and the next PDF waited for it.
- The pages of a PDF may unpack to 32 MB, which is sixteen times the largest file, and its document fails once they have unpacked to more. A file of 2 MB can hold 2 GB of packed drawing, which PDFBox reads for most of a minute, or unpacks into memory first when it is in a form that a page draws. PDFBox has no limit for either. So the contents of each page and of each form are unpacked before PDFBox is given them, and counted, and one that is drawn again counts again. Nothing of them is kept but what one way of packing hands to the next, when contents were packed more than once. A page's contents that cannot be unpacked fail the document as one that could not be read as a PDF, since they could not be counted. A reading that is refused so has ended: no thread is left behind, and the next PDF is read at once. The 30 seconds stay for what the count does not see, and for what is within it and slow all the same: a page of under 32 MB that draws a small form millions of times was read for nearly eight seconds.
- All else that PDFBox unpacks of a PDF may come to 32 MB as well, counted apart from its pages: the table of where the file's objects are, the streams that hold them, a font's files and maps, and whatever else is unpacked on the way to the text. PDFBox unpacks each of these wholly into memory, the table and some of the objects while it opens the file, and has no limit for any. They are read in many places and made in one: the document that PDFBox's parser fills makes every stream of the file. So a PDF is opened by PDFBox's parser with a document whose streams unpack themselves first, with nothing kept, and count, whenever PDFBox asks one for what it holds. A stream that is unpacked again counts again, and a form, or a page whose contents are in several parts, counts here as well as with the pages. Once they have unpacked to more, the document fails and says so, its reading has ended, and the next PDF is read at once. Files of under 2 MB whose font map, object stream, or table unpacks to 2 GB failed so within a fifth of a second, in a process of its own with the worker's heap, which Java ends [when the heap is full](0012-the-worker-runs-beside-the-api-on-the-t4g-small-with-a-limit-on-its-heap.md), and the process went on. On the local stack, a worker with that heap failed the first two of them in under a fifth of a second each, was not started again, and read the PDF after them.
- A stream that is packed as a picture is, as a JPEG or a fax, is not unpacked while a PDF is read. PDFBox takes memory for the size such a picture is said to have before it unpacks any of it, so it cannot be counted in time: a font's file of a hundred bytes that was said to be a fax of 250 MB filled a heap as large as the worker's. No text is packed so. PDFBox goes on as with any stream it cannot unpack: a font is read without its file, and a page whose contents are packed so fails the document as one that could not be read as a PDF. The same holds for a stream that is said to be packed in rows of more than a megabyte. Two of the other ways of packing can pack in rows, each told from the one above as a picture's are, and PDFBox takes memory for two rows of the length the file states before it unpacks any: a font's map of a few bytes that was said to be in rows of 256 MB filled such a heap. Shorter rows are unpacked as before: the table of where a file's objects are is often packed in rows of a few bytes.
- What is counted is what a PDF unpacks to, and not what PDFBox makes of it, so a file within the limit can still fill the heap. A font's map from codes to characters can name a range of two thousand million codes in one line, and PDFBox keeps an entry for each: a PDF of 1,175 bytes with such a map filled a heap as large as the worker's. So did a PDF of 30,554 bytes whose object stream unpacks to 31 MB, which is within the limit, and holds a list of 15.5 million empty lists, each of which PDFBox keeps as an object of its own. PDFBox has no limit for either, and the count cannot see them without reading what is unpacked as PDFBox does. The heap they fill is no longer the worker's: [a PDF is read in a process of its own](0021-a-pdf-is-read-in-a-process-of-its-own-which-java-ends-in-the-workers-place.md) with a heap of 64 MiB, which Java ends when that heap is full. The document of each of the two files then fails once, with "It takes more memory to read than a PDF may.", the worker is not started again, nothing reaches the dead-letter queue, and the PDF after it is read. The tests make both files, at about 1.2 kB and 30,545 bytes, and a test holds each. On the local stack, a worker with the host's options and restart policy showed each as failed with that reason at the page's first look, 3.1 seconds after the upload, kept the start time it had, and had the PDF uploaded next ready. Before, each ended the worker three times, two minutes apart, and its document said "Running" for six minutes.
- Still open for the two files and whatever else is of their kind: each such upload takes a process's memory, about 120 MiB at its worst on the machine it was measured on, and up to a second or two of a processor, and anyone signed in as the demo's admin can make one. A PDF that needs more than 64 MiB of heap to be read is refused, where the worker's 192 read it. The host has not been measured with such a process on it.
- A file that cannot be read fails once and is not tried three times. Only a failure that another try could end differently is the job's.
